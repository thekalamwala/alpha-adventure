Safety on a trek or trip is a shared responsibility. This page covers what we do to plan and manage safety on our end, what changes depending on the kind of trip you take, and what we need every traveller to check and prepare before joining us.

On every trek, your trek leader has the final say on pace, route and turnaround. On every backpacking trip, your trip captain holds that same authority. If a call is made to slow down, wait out weather, change route or turn back, it is made for safety, and it stands, regardless of how the group feels about it in the moment.

Our safety measures

These are the measures we build into every trek and every backpacking trip we run, regardless of region or difficulty. Region specific measures are covered separately further down this page.

Before every trek or trip

  • A pre-trek or pre-trip meeting. Before departure, we hold a meeting covering the itinerary, packing essentials, safety guidelines and code of conduct, and any questions travellers have, so that everyone starts informed and prepared.
  • Declaration and medical forms. Every traveller fills out a declaration and medical form before departure, which we review so that we are aware of any condition that could affect them on the trek or trip.

Our people and equipment

  • Trained first aid responders leading every group. Our trek leaders and trip captains are trained first aid responders.
  • Technically sound leadership on technical terrain. Sections that involve technical terrain, such as rock patches, snow, river crossings or exposed ridges, are led by leaders experienced in that terrain. The safety equipment used on such sections, including ropes and harnesses, is checked before the trek and kept in proper working condition.
  • A first aid kit on every trek, matched to the terrain. We always carry a basic first aid kit, and what it contains is changed depending on the terrain being trekked, so it is stocked for the specific risks of that route.
  • Appropriate gear provided for the terrain. Where a route needs gear beyond what a traveller carries personally, such as technical equipment for a difficult section, we provide it as appropriate to the terrain.

On the ground

  • Radios where there is no network. In areas without mobile network coverage, we use radios to stay in communication within the team and with our base.
  • A turnaround time at every base camp. Every base camp has a defined turnaround time. If the group has not reached it by then, the plan is adjusted rather than pushed to finish regardless of how long it takes.
  • Coordination with local authorities and rescue teams. Wherever an evacuation is needed, we coordinate directly with local authorities and rescue teams to move the affected person out safely and as quickly as possible.

Respecting the mountains

  • Leave No Trace, always. We follow the Leave No Trace principle on every trek, carrying out what we carry in and keeping our impact on the terrain to a minimum.

Sahyadri & Central India Treks

Sahyadri & Central India Treks bring their own set of risks, tied mostly to weather, water and rock. This is what we watch for, and what we expect from every trekker.

Weather and route planning

  • Seasonal restrictions are followed as issued. During the monsoon months, district administrations and forest departments frequently restrict or close access to specific waterfalls, dams, rivers and fort routes when rainfall or water flow makes them unsafe. We follow these orders as they are issued, even if it means changing or cancelling a planned trek at short notice.
  • Weather checked before and during the trek. Rain, wind and visibility are checked before departure and monitored through the day, since weather on Sahyadri & Central India Treks can change quickly, especially during the monsoon.
  • Timing built around daylight and weather. Treks are timed so that descents finish well before dark, and where possible before the heaviest part of any expected rainfall.

Terrain and water safety

  • Water bodies are a leading risk, not a photo opportunity. We do not enter dam backwaters, waterfall pools, rivers or streams that are not specifically part of the planned and monitored route. Water levels can rise suddenly upstream of a trail even when it is not raining where you are standing.
  • Wet rock changes everything. Rock surfaces, exposed patches and rock cut steps common on hill and fort trails become significantly more slippery when wet. Extra caution, and sometimes a change of route, is applied on such sections during and after rain.
  • Landslide and loose ground awareness. Trails that pass below loose slopes or through recently rain affected ground are approached with caution, and avoided altogether where the risk is elevated.
  • No cliff edge photography. Standing at the edge of a cliff, waterfall or viewpoint for a photograph or video is not permitted on our treks.

Group conduct on the trail

  • Stay on the marked trail. No shortcuts, no wandering off the path to explore, and no significant gap allowed between the front and back of the group.
  • Move together through risk sections. On stretches with real exposure or water risk, the group moves together and waits for everyone to clear the section before proceeding.
  • Report discomfort early. Cramps, exhaustion, dizziness or a turned ankle should be reported to the trek leader immediately rather than pushed through quietly.

Fitness, footwear and gear

  • Footwear with real tread. Trekking shoes or shoes with a grippy sole are required on all Sahyadri & Central India Treks, and are non negotiable during the monsoon.
  • Basic fitness for stepped and steep terrain. Many Sahyadri & Central India trails involve steep, sustained ascents and descents, occasionally on rock cut steps without railings. A reasonable base level of fitness and comfort with height and exposure is expected.
  • Monsoon specific gear. A rain cover for your backpack, a poncho or rain jacket, and a change of dry clothing are expected on monsoon treks, along with basic protection against leeches and biting insects common to wet forest trails.

Himalayan treks

Altitude is the defining factor on any Himalayan trek. Most serious safety issues on high altitude treks come down to how well the body is given time to adjust, so nearly everything below connects back to that.

Altitude and acclimatization

  • Gradual altitude gain. Itineraries are planned to gain altitude gradually rather than in large single day jumps, with rest days built in at key points on longer or higher treks.
  • Climb high, sleep low. Where the route allows it, a day hike to a higher point followed by a return to a lower camp for the night is used to help the body adjust before committing to sleeping at a new altitude.
  • Rest days are not optional. If the itinerary includes an acclimatization day, it is followed as planned. It is never treated as a spare day to be skipped for a faster finish.

Recognizing and responding to altitude illness

  • Symptom briefing before ascent. Every trekker is briefed on the symptoms of Acute Mountain Sickness, including headache, nausea, dizziness, loss of appetite and breathlessness beyond what the effort would normally explain, before altitude gain begins.
  • Report symptoms immediately. Trekkers are expected to report these symptoms to the trek leader as soon as they appear, rather than waiting to see if they pass on their own.
  • Descent is the response, not a discussion. Anyone showing signs of worsening altitude illness is stopped from climbing further and, if needed, brought down to a lower altitude. This decision is made by the trek leader and is final.
  • Awareness of more serious high altitude illness. Trek leaders are trained to recognise the warning signs of more serious high altitude conditions that require urgent descent and medical attention, and to act on them without delay.

Cold, terrain and environmental hazards

  • Layered clothing for temperature swings. Himalayan weather can shift from strong sun to freezing wind within the same day, so clothing is checked before departure to make sure it can be layered up or down as needed.
  • Awareness of cold injury. Trekkers are briefed on the early signs of frostbite and hypothermia, such as numbness, loss of coordination or unusual tiredness in cold conditions, and are encouraged to keep hands, feet and other extremities dry and insulated.
  • River crossings and loose or glacial terrain. Sections involving stream crossings, moraine or loose rock are crossed under the trek leader's guidance, one person at a time where needed, rather than as a group all at once.
  • Snow and technical sections supported. Where a trek crosses snow patches or a pass with any technical difficulty, this is done with appropriate footwear traction, guidance from the trek leader, and a safety line where the terrain calls for it.

Permits and documentation

  • Region specific permits. Depending on the trek, an inner line permit, a forest department permit, or clearance from a local authority may be required. Where applicable, this is arranged as part of the trek, and travellers are told in advance what identification or documents they need to carry.
  • Carry originals and copies. Permits and identification are checked at forest checkpoints on many routes, so travellers are asked to carry both original documents and photocopies.

Hydration, nutrition and daily discipline

  • Adequate fluid intake. Dehydration makes altitude sickness worse and is easy to overlook in cold, dry mountain air. Trekkers are encouraged to drink water consistently through the day rather than only when thirsty.
  • Eating enough, even without appetite. Appetite often drops at altitude, but the body still needs the fuel. Trekkers are encouraged to eat through the day even when they do not feel particularly hungry.
  • A steady, sustainable pace. Trekkers are asked to walk at a pace they can sustain rather than rushing ahead of the group, since a fast, breathless pace at altitude increases the risk of altitude illness.

Backpacking trips

Backpacking trips usually mean more time on the road, and on several routes, altitude exposure without the gradual ascent that a trek naturally provides. Both bring their own safety considerations.

Road and vehicle safety

  • Vehicles checked before long or mountainous stretches. Brakes, tyres, fluid levels and general mechanical condition are checked before any stretch involving hill roads, passes or long distances.
  • Experienced, briefed drivers. Drivers on our trips are experienced with the terrain they are driving, and are expected to drive at a pace suited to road and weather conditions, not a fixed schedule.
  • Night driving on difficult mountain roads is avoided. Long mountain stretches are planned to be covered in daylight wherever possible.

Altitude and health on the road

  • Rest before activity at high altitude. Where a trip involves arriving somewhere significantly higher than sea level, the first day is kept light, focused on rest and hydration rather than sightseeing or physical activity.
  • The same altitude illness awareness as on treks applies. The symptoms and response to Acute Mountain Sickness described in the Himalayan trekking section above apply equally to backpacking trips that gain altitude quickly by road.
  • Limited time at extreme altitude viewpoints. Where a route includes a stop at a very high altitude point, time spent there is kept short, and the group moves to lower altitude if anyone shows discomfort.

Permits and documentation

  • Restricted area permits. Certain regions require an inner line permit or similar local authority clearance for both Indian and foreign travellers. Where this applies to your trip, it is communicated in advance and arranged as part of the planning.
  • Identification checks. Valid government photo identification is required at checkpoints on many routes. Travellers are asked to carry both originals and copies at all times during the trip.

Remote travel planning

  • Fuel and supply planning. On routes with long gaps between fuel stations, towns or medical facilities, this is planned for in advance rather than assumed.
  • Connectivity gaps. Mobile network can be patchy or absent for long stretches on many backpacking routes. Trip captains carry a way to reach our base team beyond standard mobile coverage where the route calls for it, and travellers are advised to inform their own emergency contacts about likely network gaps in advance.
  • Offline navigation as backup. Digital maps are not relied on as the only source of navigation in areas with poor connectivity.

Personal safety and belongings

  • Travel as a group during activities and free time. Trip captains ask travellers to check in before heading off independently, particularly after dark or in unfamiliar areas.
  • Keep valuables and documents secure. Identification, permits, cash and electronics are kept secure and, where possible, backed up or photographed digitally in case of loss.
  • Respect local laws and customs. Travellers are expected to follow local laws and respect local customs, particularly in border areas, religious sites and other sensitive regions.

Weather and environment

  • Layered clothing for sudden weather changes. Weather at altitude can shift quickly from clear and sunny to cold and windy, so layers are recommended regardless of season.
  • Sun and altitude exposure. High altitude sun is stronger than it feels. Sunscreen, sunglasses and lip protection are recommended even on cool or overcast days.
  • Food and water precautions. Travellers are encouraged to drink treated or bottled water and to be cautious with food from unfamiliar or informal vendors, particularly in remote areas.

Before you join, please check

Safety on a trek or trip depends as much on preparation before you arrive as it does on anything we do on the ground. Here is what we ask of every traveller, grouped by when it matters.

When you book

  • Share your medical history honestly, including any history of heart, blood pressure, respiratory, joint or other conditions that could be affected by exertion, altitude or long travel.
  • Ask us directly if you are unsure whether your fitness level or experience matches the grade of the trek or trip you are considering.
  • If you are joining a high altitude or physically demanding trek or trip, consult your doctor beforehand, particularly if you have any pre existing condition.

Before you leave home

  • Carry personal medication you may need, including any doctor prescribed medicine for altitude sickness, motion sickness or an existing condition.
  • Carry a valid government photo identification. It is required for permits and checkpoints on many of our routes.
  • Consider travel insurance that covers medical treatment, evacuation and trip cancellation, and carry a copy of your policy details with you.
  • Pack according to the gear list shared for your specific trek or trip. It reflects what is actually needed to stay safe and comfortable on that route, not a generic suggestion.
  • Share the details of your trek or trip, along with our emergency contact number, with someone at home.

Once you are with us

  • Attend the safety briefing in full, even if you have done a similar trek or trip before. Routes, conditions and instructions can differ each time.
  • Report any discomfort, illness or injury to your trek leader or trip captain immediately. Early reporting is what keeps a small issue from becoming a serious one.
  • Stay with the group on the trail or during planned activities, and inform your trek leader or trip captain before heading off on your own for any reason.
  • Alcohol and intoxicants are not permitted during trekking hours or while travel is underway on any of our trips.
  • Follow the pace, route and turnaround decisions made by your trek leader or trip captain. These calls are made for your safety and are final.
